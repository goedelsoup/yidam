# A source states what it may not be used for, and that statement outlives the dialogue

Every registered source carries, explicitly, the uses it does **not** license — redistribution,
quotation beyond a length, use outside a named project, use without a community body's
agreement — alongside what it does answer. A source entry that records only what it answers
will be read as licensing everything it does not forbid.

An access condition attaches to the recording it was granted for. It is scoped to that session,
that date and that stated purpose, and it binds everything derived from it: transcripts,
examples quoted in an analysis, and any bundle or index this repository publishes.

**This is constitutional, and the flag is the point.** A non-constitutional augmentation shapes
one bootstrap and is destroyed with `samudaya/` at genesis. This rule has to survive that. The
people who agreed to these recordings are not party to the derived repository's later decisions,
and a norm that evaporates at the genesis commit protects them for exactly as long as nobody has
started working yet.

Documentary linguistics has spent two decades building this into its archives: deposits carry
access conditions, and the CARE principles for Indigenous data governance make the point that a
permission to *hold* material is not a permission to *publish* it. The common failure is not a
refusal recorded and ignored — it is a restriction agreed verbally, never written down, and
therefore invisible to everyone downstream who had no reason to suspect it existed.

`yidam samudaya-audit` flags a constitutional augmentation for review before genesis, and asks
that it be checked against the constitution's own articles. That review is a feature of this
seed, not an obstacle to it: a rule that governs a repository for its lifetime should be read by
a person once.
